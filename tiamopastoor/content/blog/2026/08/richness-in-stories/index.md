As I write this, _House of the Dragon_ (Season 3) is about to wrap up. I purposely wanted to write it _before_ the series finale aired, because it will likely be some climactic thing that they poured all their budget and energy into, and it would change my view of the show or weaken my arguments here.

Because, as I watched the rest of the season, I was just ... bored. Barely anything interesting happened. The same discussions kept repeating, in the same rooms, with the same people.

People usually point to _pacing_ or _progression_ first, as reasons for these issues. And, sure, that's often a good place to start. It baffles me how many writers think that "arguing = conflict" and just repeat the same discussion over and over. It's like the first rule of writing: every scene should _progress_ the narrative in some way. If you have repeated scenes between two people, the thing they're talking about should be evolving each time, not just exactly the same until finally something breaks / someone makes a decision. 

But I actually wanted to start somewhere else. I wanted to start with _character_, because I believe---like most writers---that character is most important. 

* The story only exists _because_ of the characters. Without them doing things and having different personalities, there is no story or conflict.
* Without characters, nobody _cares_ about this made-up story with made-up problems.

We often talk about characters being one-dimensional. When they have no defining traits, no real personality, nothing to remember them by. They don't take much action (because actions always reveal character, even if they're silly or predictable).

What people don't talk about as often is how to actually solve it. I've seen way too many writers (on e.g. YouTube) advice "beginning writers" to fill out these spreadsheets about their characters. What's their hair color? What's their favorite band? Where were they born? And this is perhaps the worst thing you could do. We don't care about a character because we know their hair color or their favorite band!

We care about fictional characters for the same reasons we care about real human beings. Because we want to _be around them_. Because we have _shared experiences_. Because we have _fond memories_ of them, making us laugh, playing a sport together, etcetera.

The most powerful pieces of media have made me almost yearn to be with the characters for a little longer. _House of the Dragon_, like most films and shows these days, does not. Most characters almost make me roll my eyes when they are on screen again. I am waiting and waiting for most of them to die already so we can just focus on the few characters that actually interest me and do interesting stuff.

It doesn't matter that the characters are well-defined. They have a distinct look, clothing style, hair color, loyalty (Blacks or Greens), role within the small council, etcetera. You could fill out all their properties, infodump lots of details about them, and it would change nothing about how interesting they are or how much we care about them. 

Because it's not _fun_ to be around them.

The characters are always solemn and serious, brooding and silent. They only talk in bedrooms or while dining together, seemingly nowhere else. Just looking at the interactions we get, it seems as if all characters hate each other, hate their own goals, and don't enjoy anything at all.

Where is the passion? The desire? The fun? The interactions? 

Why does the show tell us people care about each other, but does not _show_ them doing anything that resembles caring for each other?

Why does the show try to convince us people are amazing friends or fiercely loyal, yet they are always serious and stressed around each other?

Why should we care about characters fighting to protect their home and family, if we've never seen that family be a loving and fun home?

I give _House of the Dragon_ as an example because it's especially egregious. Almost the entire episode has this gray and solemn look, and lots of lingering shots on really really serious or sad faces, and there is absolutely no joy to be found. Writers use "but they're family" or "but they're my wife" as an easy shortcut to communicate "they care so much about each other!" But more often, it just feels like a cheap cop-out, because they never actually _show_ them caring for each other or spending quality time together.

I've been missing this richness, this lively detail, in almost everything from the past decade(s). 

This season of HotD, there's only _one_ character I liked and rooted for, and found fun to watch. It's Aegon. Why? 

* Because he has fun interactions with Larys (who is escorting him, in a way). There's a few actually funny situations/jokes that arise naturally.
* Because we follow them in a variety of day-to-day struggles. Slowly but surely this adds richness, memorable moments, a catalogue of shared experiences with these characters.
* (Because he has an actual character arc, progression with every scene, and satisfying resolution.)

I just wonder why _nobody is allowed to have fun_ in stories. No jokes. No little interactions between characters. Nothing except "the conflict".

At some point, there seems to have been a movement to make stories as efficient as possible, cutting all the "fat" (with banter, simple daily interactions between friends and family, etcetera). But not only does this create stories that people don't care about, the "efficiency" they hoped for also is rarely achieved. Mostly because they still hold on to the old structures: episodes that are one hour long, and the climax can only happen at the end. They have to fill that hour, but they can't have anything meaningful happen _before_ the big climax of course, so ... they just repeat the same argument five times. Which is as inefficient as it comes!

{{% remark %}}
I recently stumbled upon some writing books that were all about how our ideas of story as just ONE idea. This idea of three act structures with a climax near the end is just ONE way to write a story, and it's the Western-Greek-kind-of-way. Many other stories, from other cultures, are just fine having more acts, or having climaxes at random other moments---whenever the story actually calls for it.
{{% /remark %}}

The old Game of Thrones was a master of this. Lots of scenes were just fun interactions between people. That's how we came to love Ned, the Starks, and other characters. Most of all, every scene introduced some _new_ thing about these characters, it wasn't just rehashing the same joke or the same interaction over and over.

Instead of setting every conversation during dinner, set them while friends are _playing a game_ or doing _some task they often do_.

Instead of having every conversation be a big argument or main conflict, have some just about minor things. Have them disagree but without getting angry, because they love each other and they can end the conversation with their friend with a nudge and a wink.

This isn't hard! If you really want, you can write all dialogue to be happening during a dinner in the first draft. When done, go over the script once more and change every conversation to a _different location_. At work, at play, exploring, solving a daily issue, etcetera.

Playing games in our "free time" is something as old as humanity. In fact, all animals with a sufficiently long lifespan do it too. All our research suggests that people used to have _way more_ free time to play games and do experiments and have hobbies before we invented agriculture, and especially before the industrial age happened. So yes, I don't think it's actually unreasonable to ask for a joke once in a while, or to ask for characters playing a game together, even in a "serious high-fantasy adult series".

But, of course, it's not just about "humor" or "fun". I'm missing _all_ other emotions besides solemnity and anger. That's where richness (and caring about characters) comes from.

Perhaps the emotion I miss most, and the one that _should_ be easiest to incorporate in a plot, is _conviction_. Stories are nothing but characters with goals trying to attain those goals. To have a goal, you need a certain amount of conviction and motivation, a certain passion or desire for that thing.

Let it show! Let them speak passionately about this grand world they envision or their dream. Let it show, through their actions, how obsessed they are with this goal. Let them be _happy_ (whatever that means for the character) when they achieve a victory.

Instead, whenever some major victory occurs in House of the Dragon, we just get ... no reaction. Still sad and brooding. Still solemn. Still the same dark scenes with lots of silence and boring dialogue.

How _powerful_ would it have been to have our big villain with a dragon actually _smile_ and finally accept some invite to play a game, after a victory in the story? How much more impact would that victory have? How much more depth would the character suddenly achieve?

{{% remark %}}
Compare that to the original Game of Thrones where every character had a conviction. Varys wanted to serve the realm. Ned wanted to be a good king and do what was right. The Red Viper wanted justice. After a while, many people desperately wanted revenge. Because of that conviction for a goal, people were _acting_ all the time, and all the long dialogue scenes were actually tense or interesting. Despite having way more characters than House of the Dragon and way less action, it was far more interesting to watch and every big event was emotional/meaningful.
{{% /remark %}}

It's just baffling to me how writers cut out all this richness. (They only add it "accidentally" and are then surprised people like _that part_ the most, like with Aegon.) Do they not understand the idea of _contrast_? A story can't be sad if it doesn't have happy moments to compare that with. A story can't be "mature" if there are "childish" moments to contrast it with.

I recently watched the movie _Across the Spider-Verse_ and it basically underscores all of this. It's a movie made by two directors known for their improvisational comedy flicks. That movie is full of jokes, visual jokes, stupid puns, funny situations. At the same time, the movie is full of actual heartfelt moments that mean something. Both of them work because of the _contrast_. It's an animated movie that actually works for both kids and adults, like the classic movies used to do. And to top it all off, the entire movie is _so colorful_, even mixing different styles all the time. Even in our hero's "darkest moment", there is color on the screen.

Why does barely any other film or show do this? Why do they actually put in effort to _remove_ the color from the original shots!? Sometimes we get behind the scenes footage, and it's compared to the film, and I'm always like: "The original looks so much better. The movie version is just a dark gray rectangle that sucks out your soul."

Even your most badass knight, even your biggest villain, even your most serious or mature character, will do stuff besides the main plot. They'll have a hobby of sorts, or spend time with family, or play a game, or crack jokes with friends, or have some small unique habits because of their goal/conviction. _Show it._ That's what gives a character depth. That's what makes people care. And the contrast will make people really care when that character is hurt or goes into a big serious battle.

Just write a basic scene where something happens because the plot demands it in draft 1. Then, in draft 2, set it in a different location each time, and try to find _one_ new tidbit to reveal about this character each time. Have them tell _one_ joke. Have them reminisce about one thing with their friend. Have them quickly tuck their kid into bed to show they care. Have them do the dishes while they talk. It doesn't take a lot of time, but it makes a world of difference in the richness and fun of a story. (If your budget is millions per episode, then you should _absolutely_ be able to accomplish this!)

Actual depth doesn't come from grandiose speeches or really serious faces or flat-out stating some property of yours. Character depth comes from the tiny, rich, daily life details that are regularly shown or told to the viewer. And most of daily life is about simple tasks, simple ways to fill your time, telling stories or jokes, executing on your habits or small goals. The best way to show that two people are friends, is to show them doing lots of different stuff _together_ and be comfortable doing so. It is _not_ by repeatedly stating "in all the 20 years I've known you ..." and "I would die for you, my friend!"

Those were my thoughts for today,
